The shortlist

Four ideas

Ordered by how ready each one is to start.

1. Health-domain Luganda translation, released as an open benchmark + fine-tuned model

flagship — start here

General-purpose Luganda MT (including your own two En↔Lg models, and Sunbird's) is tuned for everyday language, not clinical terms, symptom checklists, drug names, or consent-form language. Build a health-domain evaluation set (you already have one to start from), benchmark your models and a fine-tuned Sunbird variant against it, and release the whole thing — dataset, benchmark, model weights — as a standalone open project.

It's the only idea where you're extending something real: two working HF models, an existing eval set, a specific fine-tuning plan already in motion, and a team that has already won grants in this exact area. Every other idea starts from zero.
Clinicians and CHWs working in Luganda who need translated health materials to actually be trustworthy — not a chatbot, a translation layer other tools (including your own #2/#4 below) can sit on top of.
Release under your own repo and name. No dependency on their review process or community goodwill — if they or Sunbird want to adopt or link to it later, that's upside, not a prerequisite.
This is your best shot at the Sunbird playbook — publish it well (clear README, a live demo, a leaderboard table), and it's the kind of artifact other African-language NLP builders actually search for and cite.
  1. Audit your existing health-domain eval set — size, source, quality — and expand it if it's under ~300 sentence pairs (WHO/Uganda MoH materials are a fast source).
  2. Run your two HF models and vanilla Sunbird through it; score with BLEU/chrF plus a human spot-check on a sample.
  3. Start the Sunbird fine-tune on the health-domain data and re-score.
  4. Publish v0: repo, README, scores table — even before fine-tuning is "done."

2. A grant and manuscript-writing copilot, built for African funder formats

A RAG assistant on Llama 3.3 70B that knows Gates/IDRC/Wellcome/Google.org proposal structures, is grounded in ACE's own past successful proposals, and drafts/critiques sections. Consensus already covers literature search — this doesn't compete with that, it picks up after it, at the writing and structuring stage.

Generic tools exist — Dr. Claw and OpenDraft both draft papers/grants with citation checking — but neither is tuned to African funder formats, local budget/ethics realities, or ACE's own proposal history. Neither appears to run fully local, either.
Local Llama 3.3 means unpublished grant ideas never leave your machines — a real concern for researchers, and something the hosted competitors can't offer.
Early-career researchers and PhD/MSc trainees on your own team first — the ones without a mentor to review a draft before submission.
Position it as a drafting aid with a human always reviewing before submission — both for research integrity and because funders read for authentic voice.
  1. Collect 10-15 of ACE's successful past proposals plus 2-3 funders' current guidelines as the RAG corpus.
  2. Build a minimal pipeline: retrieve relevant precedent + guidelines, draft one section (e.g. "Approach"), output alongside the source snippets it used.
  3. Use it yourself on the next real ACE proposal and track: did it save real drafting time, where did it need heavy correction.
Landscape checked: Dr. Claw · OpenDraft — neither is Africa-specific or local-first.

3. A guided FAIR data maturity assessment and remediation tool

The RDA's FAIR Data Maturity Model has 41 indicators; the one existing automated tool, F-UJI, covers 16 of them and reads like something built for a data librarian, not a lab lead. Build a guided version: walk a research group or health data system through the assessment in plain language, score it, then use an LLM to turn each gap into a concrete "here's what to fix and how" step.

You've sat through the confusing version of this — which model, how to evaluate, what applies to what. Building the tool that removes that confusion for the next person is a genuinely useful, personally-motivated build.
Research group leads and health-data system managers across ACE's network — non-technical enough that "self-assess your data governance" is currently out of reach without a consultant.
F-UJI covers automated technical checks; nothing wraps it (or the manual indicators it can't check) in a guided, plain-language workflow with remediation guidance.
Scope tightly to a first pilot org (your own center is the obvious one) before generalizing — 41 indicators is a lot to cover in v1.
  1. Read the RDA FAIR Data Maturity Model spec closely and pick the 10-12 indicators most relevant to a health-data-holding research center.
  2. Build a simple guided questionnaire (web form is enough for v0) that scores against those indicators.
  3. Wire an LLM step that turns a low score on any indicator into a short, specific remediation suggestion.
  4. Run it on ACE's own data practices as the first real test.
Source: RDA FAIR Data Maturity Model, Data Science Journal, 2020

4. An anomaly-detection layer for health facility reporting data (DHIS2)

A lighter-weight sibling to #3: instead of assessing an org's data governance, this flags likely data-entry errors and outliers in DHIS2 facility reporting automatically, with plain-language explanations for district health officers.

Your reaction was neutral ("okay") rather than energized — keeping it on the list because DHIS2 is real, deployed infrastructure in Uganda already, but it's the one to revisit only after one of the top two ideas is moving.
A real relationship with a district health office or the national DHIS2 team to pilot against — without that, it's a script running on synthetic data with nowhere to go.
  1. Get access to a DHIS2 demo instance and pull sample facility-reporting data via its API.
  2. Prototype a basic outlier-detection pass before pursuing any real data-access relationship.

Run #2 (grant copilot) in parallel if you have the bandwidth, since you'll want it for ACE's next live proposal anyway. #3 (FAIR maturity tool) is the next-best standalone build once #1 has a v0 out. #4 stays parked until there's a real data-access relationship to pilot it against.
